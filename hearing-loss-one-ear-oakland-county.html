Hearing loss in one ear: options and next steps

Short answer: Hearing loss in one ear can make speech in noise, sound direction, and conversations on the poorer-hearing side harder. A hearing evaluation helps identify the type and degree of loss before you choose a traditional hearing aid, CROS system, bone-conduction option, assistive technology, or another referral.

Hinderliter Hearing Services in Birmingham serves patients throughout Oakland County. This page is general education, not a diagnosis. Sudden or rapidly worsening hearing change needs prompt medical attention.

Why one-sided hearing loss can still affect daily listening

When one ear hears better, it can be easy to overlook a problem. ASHA explains that unilateral hearing loss can affect finding where sound comes from, understanding speech in noise, and overall hearing. People may ask for repeats, respond incorrectly, feel frustrated, or feel unusually tired after listening.

Hearing with two ears is not the same as simply making one sound louder. A hearing evaluation can document each ear separately, check speech understanding, and help determine whether the better-hearing ear also needs monitoring.

ASHA's unilateral hearing loss handout describes these challenges and recommends discussing hearing technologies with an audiologist.

Hearing technology options for one ear

There is no single device that fits every unilateral hearing loss. The choice depends on the hearing thresholds in both ears, speech understanding, the cause and timing of the loss, comfort, work and home listening, and your goals.

ASHA lists traditional hearing aids, CROS systems, bone-conduction devices, and cochlear implants among options that may be considered based on the amount and type of unilateral loss. NIDCD explains that prescription devices are programmed for the person's hearing loss and that bone-anchored devices are generally used for some middle-ear problems or deafness in one ear.

ASHA adult hearing-aid guidance and NIDCD hearing-aid information provide additional context.

A practical evaluation path

  1. Start with the timeline. Note when the change began, whether it was sudden or gradual, and whether it fluctuates.
  2. Test both ears. A diagnostic hearing evaluation can compare thresholds, speech understanding, and the difference between ears.
  3. Review medical warning signs. Pain, drainage, vertigo, one-sided tinnitus, or sudden change may require medical guidance before device selection.
  4. Describe real listening situations. Bring examples such as restaurants, meetings, driving, phone calls, family conversations, or sounds arriving from the poorer-hearing side.
  5. Compare a personalized plan. Ask what each option can and cannot do, how it will be adjusted, whether a trial is available, and how the better-hearing ear will be monitored.

For sudden sensorineural hearing loss, NIDCD says symptoms should be treated as a medical emergency and evaluated immediately. Do not delay medical care to shop for a hearing device.

Read NIDCD's sudden deafness guidance and FDA hearing-aid warning signs.

Communication strategies that help right away

At home and work

  • Face the speaker and get their attention first.
  • Reduce competing sound from a TV, fan, or appliance.
  • Ask for a better position when the speaker is on the poorer-hearing side.
  • Use captions or a remote microphone when the setting calls for it.

Before an appointment

  • Write down which ear is harder to hear with.
  • Bring the onset date and any recent test results.
  • List settings where you miss speech or cannot locate sound.
  • Ask how the better-hearing ear will be protected and followed.

When to seek medical guidance promptly

The FDA lists sudden, quickly worsening, or fluctuating hearing loss; hearing loss or tinnitus in only one ear; a noticeable difference between ears; ear pain; drainage; vertigo; or severe dizziness as reasons to consult a doctor or hearing professional. If any of these apply, ask for prompt guidance rather than waiting for a routine device appointment.

This educational page does not diagnose sudden hearing loss, ear disease, or any other medical condition. If symptoms are sudden or severe, contact a medical professional or emergency service.

Hearing loss in one ear FAQs

What is single-sided or unilateral hearing loss?

Single-sided or unilateral hearing loss means hearing is reduced or absent in one ear while the other ear hears better. The amount and type of loss can vary, so a hearing test is needed to understand the pattern and choose next steps.

Can a hearing aid help hearing loss in one ear?

Some people with hearing loss in one ear benefit from a traditional hearing aid. Others may benefit from a CROS system, bone-conduction device, or, for some people with complete loss, evaluation for a cochlear implant. The right option depends on the hearing test, speech understanding, medical history, and listening goals.

What is a CROS hearing aid?

CROS means contralateral routing of signals. A microphone on the poorer-hearing side sends sound to a receiver in the better-hearing ear. A CROS system does not restore normal hearing, and an audiologist should confirm whether it fits the person’s hearing pattern and daily listening needs.

Should I use an OTC hearing aid for one-sided hearing loss?

One-sided hearing loss or tinnitus is a reason to seek professional guidance rather than treating the problem as routine mild to moderate hearing loss. The NIDCD and FDA advise evaluation for noticeable differences between ears, one-sided hearing loss, sudden change, or one-sided tinnitus.

When is one-sided hearing loss urgent?

Sudden, quickly worsening, or fluctuating hearing loss, one-sided tinnitus, ear pain, drainage, vertigo, or severe dizziness needs prompt medical guidance. Do not wait for a routine hearing-aid fitting when these warning signs are present.

How can I hear better with one good ear?

Face the speaker, reduce background noise, ask people to get your attention before speaking, and position the better-hearing ear toward the speaker when possible. An audiologist can also discuss hearing technology, assistive listening devices, and monitoring for the better-hearing ear.
