Tinnitus Treatment Options

If you hear ringing, buzzing, hissing, or other sounds that others do not hear, you are not alone. Tinnitus is common, and the good news is that there are evidence-based tinnitus management options.

If you want help now, call (248) 430-4416 or request an appointment.

Start with the right evaluation

Tinnitus is a symptom, not a diagnosis by itself. A tinnitus-focused hearing evaluation can help identify hearing loss patterns and possible contributors so your plan is based on evidence, not guesswork.

  • Hearing test and case history
  • Review of noise exposure, sleep, stress, medications, and jaw/neck factors
  • Discussion of what makes your tinnitus better or worse

Evidence-based tinnitus management options

There is no single “one-size-fits-all” treatment. Most people do best with a plan that combines education, sound strategies, and support for sleep and stress.

Education and counseling

Understanding what tinnitus is (and what it is not) reduces fear and helps your brain reclassify the sound as non-threatening.

Sound therapy (including hearing aids)

Sound therapy uses everyday sound or specific sound signals to reduce the contrast between tinnitus and a quiet room. If hearing loss is present, hearing aids may support tinnitus management by improving access to speech and ambient sound.

CBT referral pathway (when appropriate)

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is one of the most supported approaches for reducing tinnitus distress. We can coordinate with your medical team when a CBT referral is a fit.

Sleep support and practical strategies

Sleep disruption makes tinnitus feel louder for many people. We can recommend practical sound and routine strategies to support sleep, alongside your physician's guidance.

When tinnitus is urgent

Contact urgent medical care for sudden hearing loss, severe dizziness/vertigo, new neurologic symptoms, or severe one-sided symptoms that appear suddenly.

Evidence and further reading

This page is general education and does not diagnose a medical condition. The AAO-HNS tinnitus guidance supports a comprehensive audiologic evaluation when tinnitus is persistent, bothersome, associated with hearing difficulty, or limited to one ear. It also describes hearing aid evaluation, counseling, and sound therapy as management options that may fit some patients.

FAQ

What are evidence-based tinnitus treatment options?

Evidence-based tinnitus management often includes a thorough hearing evaluation, education and counseling, sound therapy (including hearing aids when hearing loss is present), and referrals for CBT when appropriate.

Do hearing aids help tinnitus?

For many patients with hearing loss, hearing aids can help by improving access to sound and reducing tinnitus contrast. Some devices also include tinnitus sound support features.

What should I expect during a tinnitus evaluation?

Most tinnitus evaluations start with a case history and questions about when the sound began, what it sounds like, whether it is one-sided or pulsatile, and how it affects sleep and concentration. The clinician may examine your ears and review medications, noise exposure, and related symptoms, then an audiologist can measure your hearing and evaluate your tinnitus. If symptoms suggest an underlying medical issue, your care team may recommend medical or imaging follow-up. The steps depend on your symptoms and are not a diagnosis by themselves.

Can tinnitus go away?

Tinnitus can improve over time for many people. Even when it does not fully disappear, evidence-based strategies can reduce how noticeable and bothersome it feels.

Is tinnitus a sign of hearing loss?

Tinnitus often occurs with hearing loss, but not everyone with tinnitus has hearing loss. A comprehensive hearing evaluation can identify hearing loss and help guide the next step in management.

When should I seek urgent care for tinnitus?

Seek urgent medical care for sudden hearing loss, severe dizziness/vertigo, one-sided facial weakness, or new neurologic symptoms.
